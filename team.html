Our partner

Schola is AMD's open-source reinforcement learning plugin for Unreal Engine, and AMD wants it to work with more engines. Godot is the first port.

Alexander Cann, Member of Technical Staff at AMD, was our main contact and came to every partner meeting. TianYue "Michael" Liu, Senior Software Engineer at AMD, was our secondary contact and joined us at kickoff. Our TA, Salman Sayeed, reviewed our demos and how we worked.

How we worked

We met AMD on Friday mornings on Teams, starting with the kickoff on September 25. Every meeting has dated minutes with its decisions and action items, and Vansh carried AMD's requests back into our planning.

Trello held every task, owner and dependency, and a card only counted as done once its pull request had merged. Day to day we talked on Discord, with a channel for each deliverable and each story.

Some stories were built in pairs on shared branches, and pieces changed hands when someone else had more context. Sanjay finished the environment side of US1 on top of Isaac's agent side, Isaac took over the gRPC connector from Sanjay, and Vansh and Isaac built US15 together.

November 6Second set of stories approved, plus general observation adapters, spaces defined in code, fewer repeated warnings, and CI on Windows.

Working with AMD's code

When we needed recurrent models to store their sequence length somewhere ONNX Runtime's C API could read it, Alex changed Schola's exporter upstream. We mirrored that change in the shared exporter and removed the manual setting in Godot.

Earlier on, we waited for AMD's frozen upstream baseline, which included their policy-state refactor, before finishing our multi-agent and recurrent APIs.

Our own changes to AMD's shared code are small additions: a Godot training guide in Schola's docs, that exporter change, and a fix so RLlib episodes don't end early when agents join or leave.

What D2 taught us

Agreeing every API up front worked: seven people could build at the same time without waiting on each other.

What didn't work was sorting out dependencies between stories too late, and polishing branches before the product worked as a whole. Switching from C# to C++ also cost us time we'd planned for features.

For D3 we kept one integration branch with a runnable example, merged shared dependencies first, set up packaged-demo CI from the start, and labelled every stand-in as one.
