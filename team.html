Seven students, one partner, every Friday.

We are CSC301 Team 20 at the University of Toronto, also known as The Hard Workers. Each of us owns user stories end to end, and we met AMD's Schola team every week for the whole term.

Our partners

Alexander Cann, Member of Technical Staff at AMD, was our primary contact and joined every partner meeting. TianYue "Michael" Liu, Senior Software Engineer at AMD, was our secondary contact and helped set the project's direction at kickoff. Our TA, Salman Sayeed, reviewed our demos and process through the term.

AMD's long-term goal is to make Schola a multi-engine platform, where people keep the same Python entry point and choose their engine. We built with that in mind: same protocol, same semantics, Godot-native APIs.

How we worked

Weekly with AMD

Seven partner meetings on Friday mornings, from the September 25 kickoff to November 6. Every one has dated minutes with decisions and action items.

Check-ins with our TA

Four recorded meetings, two of them reviews of our D2 and D3 demos, with notes on scope, presentation and how graders read our history.

One board, one chat

Trello held every task, owner and dependency, and a card only moved to done once its pull request merged. Discord had a channel per deliverable and per story.

Pairs and hand-offs

Some stories were built in pairs on shared branches, and pieces changed hands when someone had more context. Our co-authored merges show it.

What each partner meeting decided

DateDecision
September 25Kickoff. A Godot-native port that reuses Schola's Python stack, with a small extensible core and training kept separate from inference.
October 2First story set approved. Reward fields on nodes would grow into a rules language, and C# would tie the plugin to Godot's .NET build.
October 9The move to a C++ GDExtension confirmed, with every story's API already in review.
October 16All API proposals merged. The upstream baseline frozen, and the set of possible agents fixed for a training run.
October 23Drop the non-blocking poll, use one step callback, reject nested environments, and read sequence length from the model.
October 30No built-in step limit, and spaces stay a single variant type. We also presented an error-handling plan where one place translates core errors into Godot errors.
November 6Second story wave approved. Generic observation adapters, spaces defined in code, fewer repeated warnings, and CI on Windows.

Collaboration in both directions

When we needed recurrent models to report their sequence length in a form ONNX Runtime's C API could read, Alex changed Schola's exporter upstream. We then mirrored that change in the shared exporter and removed the manual setting in Godot.

Earlier, at AMD's request, we waited for and synced their policy-state refactor before finishing our multi-agent and recurrent APIs.

Our own changes to AMD's shared code are small and additive: a Godot training lifecycle guide in Schola's docs, that exporter metadata copy, and a fix so RLlib episodes don't end early when agents join or leave.

What D2 taught us

Our retrospective after D2 was blunt about what went wrong, and D3 was built around the fixes.

What worked

Agreeing every API up front meant seven people could build in parallel without waiting on each other.

What didn't

Dependencies between stories were sorted out too late, and branches were polished before the product worked as a whole. The C# to C++ switch also cost us iteration time.

What we changed

One integration branch with a runnable example, shared dependencies merged first, packaged-demo CI from the start, and every stand-in labelled as one.
